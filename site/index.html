TALA's problem
Deliver a book across campus
In this sheet
How the book works

A machine has a delivery to make

A book is waiting in a campus library. Its reader is on the other side of a courtyard. Between them are a blocked walkway, an unreliable sensor, a low battery and a stream of decisions.

This is the world of TALA, a fictional delivery robot used throughout this book. TALA is not a real product or a measured experiment. It is a small case study: simple enough to calculate by hand, and rich enough to show what AI algorithms actually do.

At first TALA only needs a route. Then it must improve a schedule, anticipate another robot, act under uncertainty, learn from experience, recognize a pattern and explain its decisions. The same campus becomes eleven different mathematical problems.

How to read this atlas

Each chapter starts with TALA's problem, introduces the vocabulary, works through an example and says where the method stops being reliable. Each lab, called a plate, starts at the worked example from the text. Change an input and watch the calculation change. The Reset button always returns to the worked example.

The notation is compact. A state is written s, an action a, a probability P, and a learned parameter w or θ. Every formula comes with a table that explains each symbol. You do not need programming experience to follow the examples.

Every example uses invented teaching data. Claims about real systems and real law are kept separate and cite their sources.

Sheet
01
TALA's problem
Know what a good delivery is
Ideas
PEAS, environment types, agent types

First, define the job

TALA can move perfectly and still fail its assignment. If it delivers the wrong book quickly, speed was the wrong definition of success.

Artificial intelligence studies computational systems that perceive, reason, learn or act toward objectives. An agent receives observations through sensors and changes its environment through actuators. A rational agent chooses the action expected to perform best, given its evidence, its available actions and its performance measure. Rational does not mean all-knowing: a good decision can still have a bad outcome when the world is uncertain.

Write the task before the code

The PEAS description separates four questions that are easy to mix up.

PEAS description of TALA's delivery task
ElementTALA's delivery task
PerformanceCorrect delivery, low delay, low energy use, no collisions
EnvironmentLibrary, corridors, courtyard, people and doors
ActuatorsWheels, brakes, display and delivery compartment
SensorsCamera, distance sensors, wheel encoders and battery meter

A performance measure judges what actually happened. It is not always the same as a convenient internal reward. Rewarding TALA only for distance travelled could teach it to circle the courtyard forever. The measure must describe the job, not an easy-to-count substitute.

What kind of world is this?

An environment is fully observable when the current observation contains everything needed for the decision, and partially observable when important information is hidden. It is deterministic when a state and an action fix the next state, and stochastic when outcomes have probabilities. A delivery task is sequential because a choice now changes later options. It is dynamic if the world changes while the agent thinks.

The campus is partially observable, stochastic, sequential, dynamic and multi-agent. Positions are continuous, although a map can divide them into a finite set of places. Known and unknown describe whether the agent has the world's rules; a known model can still describe a random world.

Five agent designs, each in one line
Agent typeHow it choosesTALA example
Simple reflexA condition and action ruleObstacle close, so brake.
Model-based reflexRules plus an internal stateThe obstacle vanished, but the door is probably still busy.
Goal-basedCompares futures against a goalWhich route reaches the reader?
Utility-basedCompares how good outcomes areWhich route balances delay and battery best?
LearningImproves its own behaviour from feedbackA critic scores each delivery; the learning element adjusts the rules.

A learning agent has a performance element that acts, a learning element that improves it, a critic that scores experience and a problem generator that suggests useful exploration. These roles can sit inside a goal-based or utility-based agent; they are not exclusive boxes.

AI, machine learning and deep learning are nested ideas. Machine learning estimates patterns from data. Deep learning uses neural networks with many learned layers. Search, logic and hand-written planning rules are AI without being learned from data.

Answer B. Judge the decision by what was known when it was made, and the outcome separately. If closures were foreseeable and the model left them out, the model needs work.

02
TALA's problem
Reach the reader by the cheapest route
Methods
BFS, DFS, UCS, A*

A route is a sequence of choices

The library door closes behind TALA. The reader is across the courtyard, and the robot must choose a sequence of corridors. This is a search problem: states, actions, a transition model, an initial state, a goal test and a path cost.

A state describes the world at one moment. A search node also records how the search got there: its parent, action and cost so far. The frontier holds nodes found but not yet expanded. A set of reached states stops the search from repeating itself.

Four frontiers, four behaviours
MethodNext node takenGuarantee under the usual assumptions
Breadth-first, BFSShallowest; a FIFO queueFewest edges; complete with finite branching
Depth-first, DFSDeepest; a stackLow memory; not optimal; can follow an infinite branch
Uniform-cost, UCSLowest path cost g(n)Cheapest route when all costs are positive
A*Lowest f(n) = g(n) + h(n)Cheapest route with an admissible heuristic and correct repeated-state handling

With branching factor b and goal depth d, BFS can store about bd nodes. DFS stores only one path and its siblings. Iterative deepening repeats depth-limited DFS with growing limits, so it finds shallow goals like BFS with memory like DFS.

A map small enough to check by hand

The teaching graph has directed roads S to A costing 2, S to B costing 1, A to G costing 7, B to C costing 2 and C to G costing 2. The route through A has fewer edges but costs 9. The route through B and C costs 5. BFS finds S, A, G when A is listed before B. UCS and A* find S, B, C, G.

f(n) = g(n) + h(n)

n
a node: one place TALA could stand, reached by one particular route
g(n)
the cost already paid from the start S to n
h(n)
the heuristic: an estimate of the cost still needed from n to the goal G
f(n)
the estimated total cost of the cheapest route through n

A heuristic is admissible if it never overestimates the true remaining cost. It is consistent if h(n) ≤ c(n, n′) + h(n′) for every edge from n to n′, where c is the edge cost. Here h(S) = 4, h(A) = 6, h(B) = 3, h(C) = 1 and h(G) = 0, which is consistent.

After expanding S, A has f = 2 + 6 = 8 and B has f = 1 + 3 = 4. A* expands B, then C with f = 3 + 1 = 4, then G with f = 5. The route costs 5 and A is never expanded. With h = 0 everywhere, A* becomes UCS. Greedy best-first search uses h alone and ignores the cost already paid.

Test for the goal when a node leaves the priority queue, not when it is first generated. A costly route to G can be found before the cheap one is ready.

Why does BFS return cost 9 on this map while UCS returns cost 5?

03
TALA's problem
Find a good delivery schedule
Methods
Hill climbing, annealing, beam search, genetic algorithms, gradient descent

Sometimes the destination is the solution

TALA now has six deliveries and one charging stop. We want a good schedule, not a record of every edit used to build it. Local search moves between candidate configurations and keeps very little history.

An objective function scores a candidate, for example total travel time. A neighbour is a small change, such as swapping two stops. If the allowed changes are too small, a better schedule may be out of reach.

Climbing is easy. Knowing when to stop is hard.

Hill climbing moves to a better neighbour until none is better. That point may be a local optimum, better than its neighbours, rather than the global optimum, better than everything. A plateau is a flat region; a ridge needs a coordinated move. Random restarts try several starting points.

Our landscape has scores 1, 3, 6, 5, 4, 7, 9, 8, 6 at positions 0 to 8. From position 1, hill climbing reaches position 2 with score 6 and stops. Reaching the 9 at position 6 means going downhill first.

Lab: hill climbing on a landscape

Plate 3.1

Worked result: starting at position 1, the climber moves to position 2 (score 6) and stops.

What to notice. Start at 4 or 5 and the same rule finds the 9. The answer depends on where you begin.

P(accept) = exp(−ΔE / T)

ΔE
how much worse the new candidate is: E(new) − E(current), for a problem we minimize
T
the temperature: high early, so bad moves are often accepted; low later, so they rarely are
exp
the exponential function; the result is a probability between 0 and 1

Simulated annealing sometimes accepts a worse move to escape a trap. An improving move (ΔE ≤ 0) is always accepted. A move that is worse by 2 is accepted with probability exp(−2/5) ≈ 0.670 at T = 5, but only exp(−2/0.5) ≈ 0.018 at T = 0.5. Guarantees of finding the global optimum need very slow cooling, not a short run.

Lab: annealing with a temperature dial

Plate 3.2

Worked result: ΔE = 2 at T = 5 gives acceptance probability 0.670.

What to notice. Lower the temperature and watch the acceptance probability collapse. The run with cooling escapes position 2 and finds the 9.

A population can explore several places

Local beam search keeps k states, generates all their successors and keeps the k best. Unlike independent restarts, the candidates compete in one pool, so without some randomness the pool can collapse into near copies.

A genetic algorithm keeps a population of chromosomes, scores their fitness, selects parents, combines them with crossover and adds random mutation. For a delivery order the chromosome is a permutation, so plain one-point crossover can duplicate stops. The operator must keep the permutation valid.

Continuous improvement

w ← w − η ∇L(w)

w
the parameter being adjusted
L(w)
the loss: a number we want to make small
∇L(w)
the gradient: the direction of steepest increase of L at w
η
the learning rate: how big each step is

Take L(w) = (w − 3)², whose gradient is 2(w − 3), starting at w = 0. With η = 0.1, w goes 0.6, 1.08, 1.464 and creeps toward 3. With η = 0.9 it jumps to 5.4, then 1.08, overshooting each time but settling. With η = 1 it bounces between 0 and 6 forever. With η = 1.1 it moves further away on every step: it diverges. A zero gradient can also be a saddle point, not a minimum.

A constraint satisfaction problem needs variables that satisfy constraints, such as room schedules or N-queens. The min-conflicts heuristic moves a conflicted variable to the value with the fewest violations.

Plate 3.3

Worked result: η = 0.1 from w = 0 gives 0.6, 1.08, 1.464.

What to notice. Above η = 1 the steps grow instead of shrinking. Too large a learning rate is not faster; it fails.

Sheet
04
TALA's problem
Share a narrow passage with another robot
Methods
Minimax, alpha-beta pruning, expectimax

Another agent gets a turn

At a narrow passage, TALA cannot choose both its own move and the other robot's response. Adversarial search plans when another agent's choice changes the outcome.

A game has states, players, legal actions, transitions, terminal states and terminal utilities. In a two-player zero-sum game, one player's gain is the other's loss. The maximizing player is MAX and the opponent is MIN. Zero-sum is an assumption, not a fact about every interaction.

Minimax works backward

Write each leaf's utility for MAX. A MIN node takes the smallest child value; a MAX node takes the largest. Under action A, MIN can produce 3 or 5. Under B, 2 or 9. MIN picks 3 under A and 2 under B, so MAX picks A: max(min(3, 5), min(2, 9)) = max(3, 2) = 3. The 9 under B does not matter if the opponent can prevent it.

Skip a branch that cannot matter

Alpha-beta pruning gives the same answer as minimax with less work. α is the best value MAX can already guarantee; β is the best value MIN can guarantee. Prune when α ≥ β. After A, α = 3. Under B the first leaf is 2, so β = 2 ≤ α, and B's second leaf is skipped. Good move ordering can cut the work from O(bm) toward O(bm/2), where m is the depth.

When the tree is too deep, search stops at a depth limit and an evaluation function estimates the position. The horizon effect pushes a disaster just past the cutoff. Quiescence search looks further in unstable positions.

Chance is not an opponent

If a turn is decided by chance, use a chance node. Expectimax takes the probability-weighted average there. With equal chances, A is worth (3 + 5)/2 = 4 and B is worth (2 + 9)/2 = 5.5, so the agent picks B. The model changed, so the answer changed.

Lab: one tree, three rules

Plate 4.1

Worked result: minimax 3 (choose A), alpha-beta skips B's second leaf, expectimax 5.5 (choose B).

What to notice. Change B's second leaf to anything. Minimax and alpha-beta never change; expectimax does.

Question 1 of 2

Why can alpha-beta skip B's second leaf?

Answer B. No value of the second leaf can remove MIN's option of 2, so B can never beat A.

Question 2 of 2

If B's leaves 2 and 9 were coin flips instead of an opponent's choice, what is B worth?

Answer C. A chance node averages: 0.5 × 2 + 0.5 × 9 = 5.5.

Carry forward

Before searching harder, decide whether the next event is an opponent's move, a partner's move or a random outcome.

05
TALA's problem
Deliver now or charge first?
Methods
MDP, Bellman equation, value iteration, Q-learning

The next reward is not the whole reward

TALA can make one more delivery now or spend time charging. Charging costs something now but may allow several deliveries later.

A Markov decision process (MDP) has states S, actions A, transition probabilities T(s′ | s, a), rewards R(s, a, s′) and a discount factor γ. The Markov property says the current state holds everything needed to predict the next one. A policy π says what to do in each state.

Gt = rt+1 + γ rt+2 + γ² rt+3 + …

Gt
the return: total reward collected from time t onward
rt+1
the reward received after the next action
γ
the discount factor, between 0 and 1: how much a reward one step later is worth now

V*(s) = maxa Σs′ T(s′ | s, a) [R(s, a, s′) + γ V*(s′)]

V*(s)
the best expected return achievable from state s
maxa
choose the action a with the largest result
Σs′ T(s′ | s, a)
average over the possible next states s′, weighted by their probability
R(s, a, s′)
the immediate reward for that transition

TALA is one step from delivery. The shortcut succeeds with probability 0.8 for +10, or fails with probability 0.2 for −5: 0.8 × 10 + 0.2 × (−5) = 7. Charge costs −1 now and leads to a state currently valued at 9: at γ = 0.9, −1 + 0.9 × 9 = 7.1. Charging wins by 0.1.

The final reward is paid on entering the terminal state, and the terminal state's own value is 0. Adding a terminal value of 10 after paying +10 would count the success twice.

Lab: the discount decides

Plate 5.1

Worked result: at γ = 0.9, shortcut 7 and charge 7.1.

What to notice. Below γ ≈ 0.889 the future matters less and the shortcut wins.

Planning with a model

Value iteration applies the Bellman backup to every state again and again; in a finite discounted MDP it converges to V*. Policy iteration alternates evaluating a fixed policy and improving it. Both need the transition and reward model.

Learning without a model

Reinforcement learning learns from interaction. It gets a reward signal, not the correct action. It must balance exploration, trying alternatives, and exploitation, using what looks best. An ε-greedy agent picks a random action with probability ε and the best-known action otherwise.

Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α [r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a)]

Q(s, a)
the current estimate of the return from taking a in s
α
the learning rate: how far the estimate moves toward the target
r
the reward just received
maxa′ Q(s′, a′)
the best estimate in the next state s′; zero if s′ is terminal
[ … ]
the TD error: target minus current estimate

If Q = 2, α = 0.5, r = 1, γ = 0.9 and the best next Q is 4, the target is 1 + 0.9 × 4 = 4.6 and the new estimate is 2 + 0.5 × (4.6 − 2) = 3.3.

Q-learning is off-policy: its target uses the greedy next action even when exploration chose something else. SARSA is on-policy: it uses the action actually taken. Monte Carlo methods wait for whole episodes. A deep Q-network replaces the table with a neural network; policy-gradient and actor-critic methods learn the policy directly.

The gridworld below has 3 rows and 4 columns. TALA starts at the bottom left. The delivery cell at the top right pays +10 and ends the episode. The wet floor below it pays −5 and also ends it. Every other move costs −1, and one cell is a wall. The random seed is fixed, so the same settings always give the same run.

Plate 5.2

Worked result: one update with Q = 2, α = 0.5, r = 1, γ = 0.9, best next Q = 4 gives 3.3. After 300 episodes the greedy policy reaches the goal in 5 moves.

06
TALA's problem
A motor alarm goes off
Methods
Bayes' theorem, Bayesian networks, sampling

A warning is evidence, not a verdict

TALA's motor sensor raises an alarm. Does the motor need repair? That depends on how common faults are and how often the sensor alarms when nothing is wrong.

A random variable is an uncertain quantity, such as motor condition. A distribution gives its values probabilities that sum to one. A joint probability P(A, B) covers both events. A conditional probability P(A | B) = P(A, B) / P(B) restricts attention to cases where B happened.

P(Fault | Alarm) = P(Alarm | Fault) P(Fault) / P(Alarm)

P(Fault)
the prior: belief before the alarm
P(Alarm | Fault)
the likelihood: how often a faulty motor triggers the alarm
P(Alarm)
the evidence: how often the alarm sounds at all, faulty or not
P(Fault | Alarm)
the posterior: belief after the alarm

Suppose 2% of motors are faulty, the sensor alarms on 90% of faulty motors, and on 10% of healthy ones. Among 1,000 motors, 20 are faulty and 18 of them alarm. Of 980 healthy motors, 98 alarm. Only 18 of 116 alarms, about 15.5%, mean a fault. This is the base-rate effect.

Lab: a thousand motors

Plate 6.1

Worked result: prior 2%, sensitivity 90%, false alarms 10% gives 18 of 116, about 15.5%.

What to notice. Halve the false-alarm rate and the posterior nearly doubles. Raising sensitivity to 100% barely helps.

P(X₁, …, Xₙ) = ∏ᵢ P(Xᵢ | Parents(Xᵢ))

Xᵢ
one variable, drawn as a node
Parents(Xᵢ)
the nodes with arrows into Xᵢ
∏ᵢ
multiply one small table per variable

A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph with a conditional probability table for each node. For Rain R, Sprinkler S and WetGround W with arrows R to S, R to W and S to W, the joint is P(R) P(S | R) P(W | R, S). An arrow states a dependency in the model; it does not prove cause.

Let P(R) = 0.2, P(S | R) = 0.01, P(S | ¬R) = 0.4, and P(W) be 0.99, 0.8, 0.9 and 0 for (R, S), (R, ¬S), (¬R, S), (¬R, ¬S). Summing over the sprinkler: rain and wet is 0.2 × [0.01 × 0.99 + 0.99 × 0.8] = 0.16038, and no rain and wet is 0.8 × [0.4 × 0.9 + 0.6 × 0] = 0.288. So P(Rain | Wet) = 0.16038 / 0.44838 ≈ 35.8%. This is inference by enumeration. Variable elimination does the same sums in a smarter order.

In a chain A → B → C or a fork A ← B → C, observing B blocks the path. In a collider A → B ← C, observing B opens it: two causes of one effect become dependent. That is explaining away.

When exact inference is too expensive, sample. Rejection sampling throws away samples that contradict the evidence. Likelihood weighting fixes the evidence and weights each sample. Gibbs sampling resamples one hidden variable at a time.

TALA's problem
Track a hidden motor condition over time
Methods
Filtering, prediction, smoothing, Viterbi

The machine's condition is hidden

One alarm is ambiguous. A sequence has structure: a motor that was faulty a moment ago is probably still faulty. A hidden Markov model (HMM) links a changing hidden state to readings we can see.

It has initial probabilities π, transition probabilities T and emission probabilities O. The next hidden state depends only on the current one, and each reading depends only on the current hidden state.

Hidden states are Healthy (H) and Faulty (F), with π = [0.8, 0.2] at time 1. A High vibration reading has P(High | H) = 0.2 and P(High | F) = 0.7; Low has 0.8 and 0.3.

Healthy0.90.1
Faulty0.20.8

Filtering estimates the current state. A first High gives weights [0.8 × 0.2, 0.2 × 0.7] = [0.16, 0.14], so the belief is [0.5333, 0.4667]. Predicting: Healthy = 0.5333 × 0.9 + 0.4667 × 0.2 = 0.5733. A second High gives weights [0.11467, 0.29867], so P(Faulty) ≈ 72.3%. No transition is applied before the first reading.

Prediction without a new reading: 0.2774 × 0.1 + 0.7226 × 0.8 ≈ 60.6% faulty. Smoothing revises the past with later evidence: after both High readings, the chance the motor was faulty at time 1 rises from 46.7% to 67.7%.

The best sequence is not a list of local guesses

Viterbi decoding finds the single most likely whole sequence. For two High readings the joint scores are H,H = 0.8 × 0.2 × 0.9 × 0.2 = 0.0288, H,F = 0.0112, F,H = 0.0056 and F,F = 0.2 × 0.7 × 0.8 × 0.7 = 0.0784. Viterbi picks F,F. Its score is not a posterior; divided by the total 0.124 it is about 63.2%. Picking the best filtered state at each time would give H,F, a different answer.

08
TALA's problem
Delete a request that is probably spam?
Methods
Expected utility, expected loss, value of information

A prediction still needs a decision

TALA's message filter says a delivery request is 80% likely to be spam. Deleting it sounds obvious, until we learn that losing a real request costs far more than reviewing a spam one.

A probability predicts. Utility values an outcome. Maximum expected utility picks the action with the largest probability-weighted utility; minimizing expected loss is the same idea.

EU(a) = Σs P(s | evidence) U(a, s)

a
an action, such as deliver, review or delete
s
a possible true state, such as spam or legitimate
P(s | evidence)
how likely that state is, given what we know
U(a, s)
how good it is to take a when the truth is s

Deleting a real message costs 100 points; delivering spam costs 5. With p = P(spam), delete costs 100(1 − p) and deliver costs 5p. Delete only when 100(1 − p) < 5p, that is p > 100/105 ≈ 95.24%. At p = 0.8, delete costs 20 and deliver costs 4, so deliver wins even though spam is more likely.

Add a review that always costs 2 and resolves the message. Deliver is best below p = 0.4, review between 0.4 and 0.98, delete above 0.98. At the boundaries the actions tie.

Lab: expected loss for three actions

Plate 8.1

Worked result: at p = 0.8 deliver costs 4, review 2, delete 20. Review is best.

What to notice. The most likely class is spam, but the cheapest action is review. The costs choose, not the label.

What makes preferences coherent?

Expected-utility theory assumes preferences are complete, transitive, continuous and independent of a shared background lottery. Cyclic preferences invite a money pump: a sequence of trades, each accepted, that leaves you where you started with less. Positive affine rescaling U′ = aU + b with a > 0 keeps the ranking of actions; other changes may not.

Information has a price

The value of perfect information is the expected value when the truth is revealed before acting, minus the best value without it. At p = 0.8 with review available, the best expected loss is 2. Perfect information would make it 0, so it is worth 2 loss points. Paying 3 for it would be worse than reviewing.

Imperfect information can help too. The best value without a forecast is 70 utility points. A forecast gives two signals with probabilities 0.6 and 0.4, and the best values after them are 90 and 50. With the forecast: 0.6 × 90 + 0.4 × 50 = 74 points, so the forecast is worth 4 points before its cost. Calling it 74% would be a unit error.

Let the data change the rule

A fixed rule can mark every message containing "urgent" as spam, and it will also reject real urgent messages. Machine learning fits a model from examples.

A feature is an input measurement, a label is the target in supervised learning, and parameters are learned values. A hyperparameter, such as tree depth, is chosen around the training. Supervised learning uses labels: classification predicts a category, regression a number. Unsupervised learning finds structure without labels.

Estimate a parameter

With coin flips H, H, H, T, maximum likelihood (MLE) picks the θ that makes the data most likely: 3/4. MAP also uses a prior. A Beta(2, 2) prior plus these flips gives a Beta(5, 3) posterior, whose mode is (5 − 1)/(5 + 3 − 2) = 2/3 and whose mean is 5/8.

score(c) = P(c) ∏ᵢ P(xᵢ | c)

c
a class, such as spam or legitimate
P(c)
the prior for the class
P(xᵢ | c)
how likely feature i is in that class, assuming features are independent given the class

Spam prior 0.4 with two clues each at 0.75 gives 0.4 × 0.75 × 0.75 = 0.225. Legitimate prior 0.6 with clue likelihoods 1/6 and 2/6 gives 0.0333. Normalized, P(spam) ≈ 87.1%. Correlated clues get counted twice. Laplace smoothing adds pseudocounts so an unseen feature does not force a zero.

Lines and probabilities

Linear regression predicts ŷ = wᵀx + b and is often trained on mean squared error, MSE = (1/n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)². For points (1, 8), (2, 11), (3, 15), (4, 18), the line 3x + 5 has MSE 0.5; least squares gives 3.4x + 4.5 with MSE 0.05. Logistic regression passes a linear score through the sigmoid σ(z) = 1/(1 + e−z) to get a probability; despite its name it is a classifier.

A decision tree splits examples by feature tests, aiming for purer groups measured by entropy −Σ p log₂ p or Gini impurity 1 − Σ p². Deep trees memorize. A random forest averages many trees trained on bootstrap samples.

Clusters without labels

K-means repeats two steps: assign each point to its nearest centroid, then move each centroid to the mean of its points. With points (1, 1), (1.5, 2), (3, 4), (5, 7), (3.5, 5), (4.5, 5), (3.5, 4.5) and starting centroids (1, 1) and (5, 7), the first update gives (1.83, 2.33) and (4.13, 5.38). Point (3, 4) then switches cluster, and the second update gives (1.25, 1.5) and (3.9, 5.1), where it stops. The final within-cluster squared distance is 8.525. PCA is different: it reduces dimensions, it does not cluster.

Test the model honestly

A training set fits parameters, a validation set chooses settings and thresholds, and a test set estimates final performance. Fit scaling and feature selection on training data only. Overfitting learns noise. Data leakage lets future information into training. Distribution shift means new data differ from old.

Take spam as positive: TP = 8, FP = 12, FN = 2, TN = 978 out of 1,000. Accuracy (TP + TN)/N = 98.6%, but precision TP/(TP + FP) = 40% and recall TP/(TP + FN) = 80%. F1 = 2PR/(P + R) ≈ 53.3%. A model that calls everything legitimate scores 99% accuracy and 0% recall.

A boundary can learn to bend

TALA's gripper senses contact and slip. We want to know if the book is held securely. A perceptron multiplies inputs by weights, adds a bias and applies a threshold.

z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b; ŷ = 1 if z ≥ 0, otherwise 0

x₁, x₂
inputs: contact and slip, scaled to 0 to 1
w₁, w₂
weights: how much each input counts
b
the bias: shifts the boundary
ŷ
the predicted class: 1 secure, 0 insecure

With w₁ = 2, w₂ = −3 and b = −0.5, contact 0.8 and slip 0.2 give z = 2 × 0.8 − 3 × 0.2 − 0.5 = 0.5: secure. Slip 0.8 gives z = −1.3: insecure. The line 2x₁ − 3x₂ − 0.5 = 0 is the decision boundary.

The update rule is w ← w + η(y − ŷ)x and b ← b + η(y − ŷ). For x = [0.8, 0.8] with label 1 but prediction 0, and η = 0.1, the weights become [2.08, −2.92] and the bias −0.4.

Plate 10.1

Worked result: contact 0.8, slip 0.2 gives z = 0.5, secure.

What to notice. The bias slides the line without turning it. The weights turn it.

The XOR wall

The perceptron convergence theorem assumes the classes can be split by a line. XOR cannot: (0, 0) and (1, 1) are class 0, (0, 1) and (1, 0) are class 1. No single line separates them, so training never settles.

A multilayer perceptron adds hidden units. One hidden unit computes OR, step(x₁ + x₂ − 0.5); another computes AND, step(x₁ + x₂ − 1.5); the output computes step(h₁ − h₂ − 0.5), which is "OR but not AND", exactly XOR. The hidden layer changes the representation, and in the new one a line is enough.

Without a nonlinear activation function, stacked layers collapse into one affine map, so depth alone cannot solve XOR. ReLU is max(0, z); sigmoid maps to (0, 1); tanh maps to (−1, 1).

Lab: XOR, one layer against two

Plate 10.2

Worked result: the perceptron always misclassifies at least one XOR point. The hand-set two-layer net outputs 0, 1, 1, 0, and a trained 2-2-1 sigmoid net learns the same.

What to notice. Drag the line anywhere: at least one point is always wrong. Train the small network and its loss falls toward zero.

Learning from an error signal

A forward pass computes the output, a loss measures the mismatch, backpropagation uses the chain rule to get each parameter's gradient, and an optimizer such as gradient descent changes the parameters. Backpropagation computes gradients; it is not the optimizer. An epoch is one pass over the data; a mini-batch is a subset used for one update.

Regularization penalizes complexity, early stopping halts when validation error rises, and dropout randomly drops activations during training. None of them fixes wrong labels.

When the input is an image

A convolutional neural network (CNN) slides small learned filters across the image with shared weights. Early layers react to edges; later layers combine them into larger patterns. Pooling or strides shrink the map, and a final head outputs the class. Recognizing a book does not mean understanding it, and lighting or background changes can break it.

The model leaves the notebook

TALA now works around people. Its camera can capture faces. Its language interface can make a confident mistake. Its operator needs to know who maintains it, who can stop it and what happens when it fails.

A test score measures behaviour on a dataset. It does not decide whether a real deployment treats people, data and accountability well.

Kazakhstan's framework

The legal lecture centres on Kazakhstan's Law No. 230-VIII of 17 November 2025, "On Artificial Intelligence". The official consolidated text is the reference for exact wording; Adilet labels its English version an unofficial translation. This chapter describes concepts at course level and does not decide the legal status of any real system.

The framework separates risk level from autonomy level. Risk is about possible impact in the context of use; autonomy is about how independently the system acts. The lecture describes minimal, medium and high risk, and low, medium and high autonomy. A simple rule in a high-stakes setting can need more care than a clever model used for something trivial.

Risk and autonomy are separate axes

Make the system legible

The law addresses transparency in AI use and synthetic results. A person should be able to tell when an AI system is involved and understand its limits. The legal definition of a synthetic result must be read precisely, not stretched to every generated sentence. For TALA: say it is automated, say what it cannot determine, offer a way to reach a person and keep a record of important actions.

The data do not disappear inside the model

Personal-data protection, information security and intellectual-property law still apply next to the AI law. Taking a camera image, sending it to a remote service and keeping it for training are three different operations, each needing a purpose and a basis. Running locally reduces some transfers but does not prove compliance. For TALA: keep camera footage briefly, restrict access to logs and document any third-party processing.

Authorship, audits and ownership

The lecture highlights human creative contribution in copyright. Generated output is not automatically evidence of authorship. The law discusses AI audits, including for trusted-system lists; do not assume every system needs the same audit. Name an owner for maintenance, for monitoring errors and for handling complaints.

A bias is a systematic tendency that can come from data, labels or the objective. A hallucination is a fluent, unsupported or false output presented as fact. The course also introduces the Digital Qazaqstan strategy through 2029; a strategy states targets, not achieved results.
